Our Story

Two brothers, one Youniverse.

Youniverse started in 2015 on a single street in Dadar West, Mumbai — a shirt hand-painted because a plain one felt unfinished. Ten years on, it's still run the same way: designed, painted, patched and stitched in-house, by people who know the fabric by hand.

The label is owned and run by brothers Mahesh Nishar and Amit Nishar, who still work out of the same neighbourhood the brand was born in.

“Every shirt we've ever sold was made an arm's length from where we're standing. That's not changing — we're just taking it further.” Mahesh & Amit Nishar, Founders

Youniverse is stocked with retailers pan-India today. The next chapter is bigger: Mahesh and Amit are working to carry the same hand-painted, in-house-made philosophy to customers outside India, while keeping every piece rooted in Dadar.

The Journal

Stories from the shop floor.

Notes on how the clothes actually get made, from the Dadar workshop.

Craft

Why We Still Hand-Paint Every Shirt

No two Youniverse shirts are ever quite the same — and that's on purpose.

Read the story
Every Youniverse print starts as a brush, not a machine. Before a shirt is cut, the fabric is laid flat across a workbench in our Dadar workshop and painted panel by panel — a butterfly here, a Warli dancer there, a line-drawn face on a sleeve. It's slower than screen printing, and it means every batch has small, honest variations: a stroke that runs slightly heavier on one cuff, a motif that sits a centimetre higher on the next. We've kept it this way since 2015 because it's the whole point of the label. A machine print looks the same on the thousandth shirt as it did on the first. A hand-painted one doesn't — and that's what makes it feel like it belongs to the person wearing it, not the person before them. It also means every piece employs a pair of hands in our own workshop, not a subcontractor three cities away. So if your Youniverse shirt looks a little different from the one in the shop photo, that's not a defect. That's the brushstroke.

Process

Wide-Leg, Zero Off-Cuts

Our patchwork denim isn't a trend print — it's what's left over from everything else we cut.

Read the story
Wide-leg denim uses more fabric than almost anything else we make, which used to bother us — until we started using the panels differently. Every Youniverse patchwork jean starts as offcuts: the leftover denim from a previous season's cutting room floor, sorted by wash and weight, then pieced back together into a single wide leg before it's painted or embroidered. It's more work than cutting a pair from a single bolt of denim — every panel has to be matched and seamed by hand before the leg even takes shape — but it means very little of the denim that enters our workshop leaves it as waste. The tribal Warli panels and portrait prints you see on our jeans aren't hiding the patchwork underneath; they're painted straight onto it, so the seams stay part of the design instead of something to disguise. It's not marketed as a sustainability line. It's just how we've always cut denim in Dadar — we'd rather piece it together than throw it out.

Future

From Dadar to the World

Pan-India was the first chapter. Mahesh and Amit Nishar on what comes after.

Read the story
Youniverse has spent ten years as a Mumbai label first — sold out of a single shop in Dadar West, then slowly picked up by retailers across the country until the brand was stocked pan-India without ever opening a second flagship. That was never the ceiling, according to founders Mahesh and Amit Nishar. "Every market we've entered so far has been because a retailer found us, not the other way around," says Amit. "The next step is doing that on purpose — outside India." Mahesh adds that the plan isn't to change what Youniverse is to get there: "The hand-painting, the in-house cutting, the fact that a customer can still ask us for a custom size — that's not going to scale down. If anything, that's the part we think travels best." For now, that means growing the wholesale side, taking more custom and special-order requests directly from customers, and slowly introducing the label to retailers beyond India — while keeping every piece painted and stitched in the same Dadar workshop it always has been.
